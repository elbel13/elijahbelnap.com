In March I was diagnosed with ADHD inattentive type. I wasn't terribly surprised; my brother Ben and I had discussed ADHD extensively and I had begun to think it was a defining factor in why I experience certain challenges. However, this late diagnosis wasn't because ADHD appeared out of nowhere nor because it never caused me any issues, but rather because I've had to learn to adapt over the years to function despite having ADHD. Had I recognized my ADHD sooner I perhaps I could have avoided learning some lessons.

This ADHD awareness month I have been reflecting a lot on my ADHD experience. While those affected by ADHD may or may not relate to certain aspects of my experience, my hope is that by sharing some of my thoughts I can help raise awareness of issues that may arise from ADHD, how they manifest in the workplace, and how you can help yourself or your coworkers have a little less pain trying to deal with them.

## What inattentive Type ADHD Feels Like For Me
### The Token Analogy

The analogy that I find best suites what living with inattentive type ADHD is like is what I would like to call "The Token Analogy." I know that tokens have taken on a new meaning in the AI era, but for this analogy the tokens we refer to are more similar to arcade tokens. I'm sure many of us remember going to Chuck E' Cheese and, at a discounted package price, being alloted our share of tokens.

In my case, once alloted tokens for a trip, there was no "getting more." Since my tokens were limited, I spent a lot of time paying attention to how many tokens each game cost. Sometimes I would focus on games that had an optimal tokens to ticket ratio, or gave me the most playtime, rather than the games that I thought looked most attractive. In short I had to spend my tokens wisely because they were a limited resource, and once the tokens were gone I wouldn't be able to participate in additional arcade activities.

I've learned to ration my attention the same way I would ration the tokens at an arcade. Focusing on daily tasks takes effort and I like to refer to the effort needed as "focus tokens". Like in an arcade, not all tasks cost the same number of tokens. Spending an hour on the big project? That might be a lot of focus tokens. Sending a message over Slack to follow up with someone? A smaller number of tokens. There are important tasks and deadlines, but because my ability to focus is limited I have to be strategic on which ones I choose to do when.

Some days I feel like I have a lot of focus tokens, or perhaps there's a task that's particularly interesting to me. On those days I seem to be incredibly productive and can get a lot of important work done. Other days, I feel like I have barely any focus tokens at all and many tasks seem impossible to even start. To deal with this, on those days I choose to do tasks that require less focus but still need doing.

While this strategy usually works for me, I can understand that not everyone has this flexibility. Many people don't have a long list of tasks requiring varying degrees of focus nor do they have the ability to choose between them. For those with ADHD, I imagine that many individuals find it nearly impossible to start many of the important tasks that their role requires, or perhaps they feel they never have enough focus tokens to make it through the day. I've spoken to others with ADHD that find medication or taking an exercise break to be helpful ways to obtain additional focus tokens.
